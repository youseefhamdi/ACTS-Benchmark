## Abstract

**Background.** Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed for automated cryptographic forensics, including cipher family identification from raw ciphertext. Prior work has reported accuracy rates exceeding 85%–92%, raising the possibility that LLMs have learned implicit cryptanalytic capabilities from their training data. However, existing evaluations have not controlled for **metadata leakage** — the tendency of LLMs to exploit filename conventions, header structures, or auxiliary contextual cues rather than analyzing the ciphertext itself.

**Method.** We present ACTS v2 (Artifacts in Cipher Testing Suite), a **reproducible benchmark protocol** designed to isolate cryptanalytic ability from metadata dependence. Our methodology introduces (1) a 140-file corpus with five-dimensional randomization (plaintext, key, IV, padding, implementation); (2) a tiered evaluation framework (Tier-1: full metadata; Tier-3: completely blind; Tier-5: forced chain-of-thought/code/self-correction reasoning); and (3) rigorous statistical controls including Wilson confidence intervals, McNemar paired tests, and stratified train/test separation.

**Results.** In a real-world validation using **581 total analyses** (161 live LLM inferences across five model–backend combinations + 420 deterministic heuristic analyses) from three providers (Google, OpenAI, NVIDIA) at zero cost, we observe a **mean metadata gap of 75.0 percentage points** (Tier-1: 100%; Tier-3: 25.0%). Blind accuracy ranges from 3.6% to 57.1% depending on the model and inference backend — never approaching the expert-level performance claimed in prior work. Critically, the same model (Nemotron Super) achieves 57.1% accuracy on one backend (Ollama) but only 3.6% on another (OpenRouter), demonstrating that apparent "cryptanalytic ability" is backend-dependent and not a property of the model itself. All models exhibit distinct frequency biases when blind: Gemma 4 hallucinates ChaCha20 (85.7% of guesses), GPT-OSS fixates on AES-256 (50%), and Nemotron on OpenRouter fixates on AES-128 (78.6%).

To test whether forced reasoning could overcome these limitations, we executed Tier-5: three formats of mandatory explicit reasoning (chain-of-thought, code-as-reasoning, self-correction) applied to the full 140-file corpus. **Result: 46.4% accuracy — identical across all three formats.** This ceiling is entirely explained by trivial file-size heuristics (RSA-2048 = 256B, ML-KEM-768 = 1088B, AES-128 = mod16==0 default), not by genuine cryptanalysis. The executing system self-admitted: "This is a simple deterministic heuristic engine disguised as reasoning." The 46.4% exceeds the theoretical size-only ceiling (32.1%) by 14.3 percentage points due to biased stereotyping, not understanding.

To establish a rigorous control baseline, we trained classical machine learning classifiers (Tier-6: Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Linear SVM). On the 140-file pilot corpus with 25 features, **Random Forest achieved 42.9% overall — random-guessing (15–25%) on all five symmetric ciphers, confirming no linearly separable signal.** On the expanded 700-file corpus with ~30 features, non-linear RF discovers a **61.6% artifact signal**, proving the discriminative information is structured non-linearly — a structure LLMs miss. On the pilot, the Tier-5 LLM's 3.5 pp 'advantage' over RF (46.4% vs 42.9%) is **not evidence of understanding** — it is a **failure mode** (biased stereotyping/confabulation). On 700 files, the gap reverses to ML +10.2 pp.

**Conclusions.** These results empirically establish that LLM cipher identification is **metadata-dependent and information-theoretically bounded**, not cryptanalysis-based. Forcing explicit reasoning does not create understanding where none exists — it only dresses deterministic heuristics in plausible language. Classical ML control shows the pilot ceiling is ~43% with 25 features (no linear signal), while the expanded corpus reaches 61.6% with non-linear ML — confirming the signal is structured non-linearly. LLMs remain below even this ceiling (max 51.4%), demonstrating the 10.2 pp confabulation tax from missing actual data patterns. The only reliably identifiable ciphers in blind mode are structurally distinctive ones (RSA-2048, ML-KEM-768); statistically uniform symmetric ciphers (AES, ChaCha20, 3DES) remain effectively opaque. ACTS v2 provides the methodology, dataset, and evaluation framework for reproducible validation of future claims in this domain.

**Keywords.** Large language models, cryptographic forensics, cipher identification, benchmark protocol, metadata leakage, reproducibility
